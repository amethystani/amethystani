# Which starting hands to play

![Two hole cards face up: the ace of spades and the king of spades.](https://amethystani.github.io/amethystani/learn/starting-hands-hero.png)

Play about one hand in eight when you are first to act, and about two in five when you are last. There are 169 distinct starting hands in Texas Hold’em and most of them are not worth playing, but which ones qualify depends on where you are sitting, because acting last is worth more than almost any pair of cards.

That is the answer, and it is why the chart below is arranged by position rather than by strength. Most starting-hand charts tell you _what_ to play. The useful question is _when_.

That works out at **13% of hands from early position, 20% from the middle, and 41% from the button.** The set roughly triples between the first seat and the last, and the cards did not change. Your seat did.

**Read this chart as a floor, not a law.** It assumes nobody has raised in front of you, a six-handed table and stacks of a normal depth. It is a conventional beginner range rather than anything solved, and any strong player would adjust it constantly. It is a good place to start and a bad place to stay.

## Why position changes the answer at all

You are not being asked to guess whether your two cards are good. You are being asked whether they will still be good after four rounds of betting against people you cannot see.

Acting last means you have watched everyone else act before you decide. You get more information for the same money, every round, for the whole hand. That is worth more than most of the difference between one starting hand and another, which is why a hand like J9s is a fold from the first seat and a routine open from the button. [The position guide](https://amethystani.github.io/amethystani/learn/position/) is the long version.

The other half of it is how many people are left behind you. From early position five players still get to wake up with a better hand. From the button there are two. The same cards are simply likelier to be best when fewer people can beat them.

## What actually makes a hand worth playing

Four things, and they matter in this order.

**High cards.** Most hands never improve past one pair, so the pair you make wants to be a big one. This is the whole reason ace-king beats king-queen and king-queen beats queen-jack. Nothing clever in it.

**Being a pair already.** You start with a made hand nobody has to help you find. Pocket pairs are rare, at 5.9% of hands, and small ones are worth playing mostly because of what they can become: a pocket pair flops a set or better **11.8% of the time**, which is once in every eight and a half hands. That is the whole business case for playing 44.

**Cards that work together.** Connected cards make straights, and cards close in rank make more straights than distant ones. 76 belongs to four possible straights. Put one gap between them and 86 belongs to three. King-seven belongs to none at all, because no run of five ranks holds both.

**Being suited.** This one is real and it is smaller than people think. Two suited cards make a flush by the river **6.4% of the time**, so a flush is not the plan, it is a bonus. Priced properly:

| Hand | Equity against a fixed opponent | What the suits are worth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A♠K♠ against Q♣Q♦ | 46.2% |  |
| A♠K♥ against Q♣Q♦ | 42.8% | 3.4 points for the suits |
| 7♠6♠ against T♣T♦ | 21.6% |  |
| 7♠6♥ against T♣T♦ | 17.8% | 3.8 points for the suits |

**So suitedness is worth about three to four points. It is a tiebreaker, not a transformation.** It is usually enough to move a hand one band on the chart above, which is exactly what it does: ATs is playable from anywhere and ATo waits for the middle. It is not enough to make two low suited cards worth playing, and “but they were suited” has probably cost more beginners more money than any other sentence in poker.

## The four traps

**Any ace.** A5o and A9o are on the chart from late position only, and for a reason. When your ace pairs, you have top pair with a bad kicker, and the way that hand loses is to somebody with the same pair and a better one. It wins small pots and loses big ones. Ace-rag is the most reliably overplayed hand in poker, and it is [kickers](https://amethystani.github.io/amethystani/learn/hand-rankings/) doing the damage.

**Any two suited cards.** 6.4%. See above.

**Face cards that do not match.** KJo, QJo and JTo look like a handful of picture cards. They make second-best pairs against exactly the hands that raise, which is the definition of an expensive holding. Note where they sit on the chart, which is later than they feel.

**Playing a hand because you are bored.** Most of poker is folding. From the first seat this chart folds 87% of the time, and the discipline to do that is worth more than any refinement to which hands are in the other 13%.

## How often you actually get the good stuff

Worth knowing, because it sets expectations for a session and stops the chart feeling unfairly tight.

| You are dealt | How often | Roughly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Any pocket pair | 5.88% | 1 in 17 hands |
| Aces, kings or queens | 1.36% | 1 in 74 |
| Ace-king, suited or not | 1.21% | 1 in 83 |
| A named pair, say aces | 0.45% | 1 in 221 |
| A named suited hand, say ace-king suited | 0.30% | 1 in 332 |

Aces arrive once in 221 hands. At a normal pace that is a couple of hours. **The tight-looking chart above is not asking you to wait for aces, and if you sit waiting for aces you will pay far more in blinds than they will ever win you.** Most of the money is made with hands that are merely good, in seats where being merely good is enough.
